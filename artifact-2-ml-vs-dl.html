Title

ML or Deep Learning? A Decision Guide for Insights Teams

Introduction

When a research or analytics team decides to “use AI” on a problem, one of the first real choices is whether a traditional machine learning model is enough or whether the problem needs deep learning. The wrong choice wastes time and money: deep learning on a small survey table adds cost and makes results harder to explain, while traditional models cannot handle raw images, text or speech. This artifact turns my Workshop 2 report on machine learning versus deep learning into a short guide that helps insights teams make that choice.

Description

The guide has three parts: a four-question decision flow, a side-by-side comparison of the two approaches, and two real-world cases from published research. The first case is customer churn prediction with a support vector machine (traditional ML). The second is Google’s neural machine translation system (deep learning). For each case, the guide explains why the chosen approach fits and why the other one does not. The full written report is available as a PDF.

The decision guide

Four questions to ask before choosing

Rule of thumb: if most answers fall in the left column, start with traditional ML. Move to deep learning only when the data is raw and plentiful and the extra cost is justified.

How the two approaches differ

Traditional MLDeep learning
Who finds the featuresPeople choose them (e.g., contract length, payment history)The neural network learns them from raw data
Data neededWorks with smaller datasetsNeeds large datasets
ComputingModest; fast to train and retrainHeavy; usually needs GPUs
Explaining resultsEasier: based on named variablesHarder: many layers and parameters
Typical insights usesChurn and retention, key-driver analysis, segment scoringReading open-ended text at scale, image and video analysis, translation

Two real-world cases

Traditional ML · support vector machine

Predicting customer churn

Coussement and Van den Poel (2008) built SVM churn models for a newspaper subscription service and compared them with logistic regression and random forests. Tuning mattered: the SVMs beat logistic regression only with the better of two parameter-selection methods, and random forests did best overall.

Why ML fits: structured customer records with meaningful fields, a manageable dataset, and results a retention team has to understand and act on.

Why not deep learning: it needs far more data, is harder to explain, and has little to discover when the useful information is already in a few known fields.

Deep learning · neural translation

Translating between languages

Wu et al. (2016) describe Google’s Neural Machine Translation system: a deep LSTM network with eight encoder and eight decoder layers, plus attention. In human evaluations it cut translation errors by about 60% on average compared with Google’s earlier phrase-based system.

Why deep learning fits: the input is raw language, where meaning depends on word order and context that no team could list by hand.

Why not traditional ML: hand-made features such as word counts lose word order, and a classifier predicts one label instead of producing a whole sentence.

Objective

The Workshop 2 assignment asked me to choose one traditional machine learning example and one deep learning example from the lesson, find a real-world application of each, and explain why each approach suits its problem and why the other does not. For the portfolio, my goal was to turn that analysis into something an insights or analytics team could use before starting a project: a short way to decide which approach a problem needs.

Process

  1. Picked two lesson examples that sit at opposite ends: churn prediction, where data is structured, and machine translation, where data is raw language.
  2. Found published real-world applications for each and checked the details against the original sources.
  3. Wrote the report (2.3) explaining why each approach fits and why the other does not.
  4. Adapted it for the portfolio audience: turned the reasoning into a four-question decision flow and a comparison table, and kept the two cases as short evidence panels.
  5. Added typical insights uses so a research team can connect each approach to work it already does.
  6. Reviewed the page against the course artifact template and the 2.5 rubric.

Value Proposition

This guide helps insights and analytics teams decide in a few minutes whether a problem needs traditional machine learning or deep learning, so they avoid paying for complexity they do not need and can explain their choice to stakeholders.

Unique Value

Most explanations of machine learning versus deep learning focus on how the algorithms work. This guide starts from the decision a project team actually faces and frames it in terms that research teams use every day: the kind of data, how many cases there are, and whether the client needs to understand the “why.” Those are the same questions that come up when scoping a market research study, which is where my experience comes from.

Relevance

This artifact shows a different skill from my first one. Artifact 1 placed AI developments in context; this one applies technical understanding to a practical choice. It supports my personal value proposition of bringing AI into research carefully: choosing the simplest method that answers the question and being able to explain why. It is relevant now because many teams are under pressure to use advanced AI even when a simpler model would do the job better.
