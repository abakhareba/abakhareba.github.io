Title

Neural Networks in Plain Words: A Beginner’s Explainer for Research Teams

Introduction

Insights teams are now asked to explain AI to people who have never studied it: clients, brand managers and new team members. If they cannot explain a model simply, people either distrust it or trust it too much. This artifact comes from my Workshop 3 discussion, where the task was to create the best simple explanation of neural networks I could, using a generative AI tool and the course’s strategies for communicating for learning. I adapted it into a short explainer that a research team can reuse when it needs to introduce neural networks to a non-technical audience.

Description

The explainer teaches what a neural network is in four short steps, built around one everyday story: learning to make tea the way a friend likes it. A simple diagram shows how information moves through the layers, and a one-question check lets the reader test their understanding. Below it, a short guide suggests how a team could use the explainer with clients and new analysts, and a comparison shows how the wording changed from my first version to the simpler final one.

The explainer

A neural network learns the way we learn to make tea

Think about learning to make tea for a friend. The first cup is too sweet, so you use less sugar next time. The second cup is too weak, so you let it brew longer. After a few tries, you get it just right. A neural network learns in the same way: try, check, fix, repeat.

1
It is made of many tiny helpers.

Each helper (called a “neuron”) looks at one small clue and passes a message to the next helpers. The helpers are lined up in rows called layers.

2
Messages move from row to row.
3
It learns from its mistakes.

Each link between helpers has a strength, like the amount of sugar in the tea. When the answer is wrong, the network changes these strengths a little. After thousands of tries, it gets the answer right most of the time.

4
You already use it.

When your phone suggests the next word while you type, or a video app suggests what to watch next, a neural network is at work.

Quick check: If the network calls an orange an apple, what does it change? (click to see the answer)

The strength of the links between its helpers, like changing the sugar in the tea.

How a research team could use it

Client briefings. Open a methods discussion with the tea story before explaining a model built on neural networks, such as one that codes open-ended survey answers.
Onboarding new analysts. Use the four steps as a first introduction, then add technical terms (input layer, weights, activation functions) once the basics are clear.
Setting expectations. Step 3 makes a useful point for stakeholders: a network learns from examples and still gets some answers wrong, so its results need checking.

Objective

The Workshop 3 discussion asked students to apply the lesson’s communication strategies while working with a generative AI model to create the best simple explanation of neural networks, and to disclose the tools used. For the portfolio, my objective was to turn that explanation into something reusable: a short explainer an insights team can show a non-technical audience, plus guidance on when and how to use it.

Process

  1. Chose the audience: a complete beginner with no technical background.
  2. Prompted a generative AI tool (Claude) to explain neural networks using the lesson’s strategies: small chunks, an everyday example, one idea at a time and a quick check at the end.
  3. Reviewed the first version and found it still used technical terms such as “input layer” and “hidden layers.”
  4. Adjusted the prompt to ask for simpler words and different examples, which produced the tea version.
  5. Checked the content against the Workshop 3 lesson on deep learning and neural networks (layers, neurons, and learning by adjusting connection strengths).
  6. Posted it to the class discussion with a short explanation of the strategies used.
  7. Adapted it for the portfolio: added the layer diagram, a click-to-reveal check question, a guide for research teams and the first-draft-to-final comparison.

Value Proposition

This explainer gives research and insights teams a ready, tested way to introduce neural networks to clients and new colleagues in a few minutes, so conversations about AI-based methods start from shared understanding instead of jargon.

Unique Value

Most beginner explanations of neural networks are written for students of technology. This one is written for the people a research team presents to. It uses one story from start to finish, shows how the wording was tested and simplified, and adds practical guidance on where it fits in client and onboarding conversations. That reflects my background: years of presenting research results to stakeholders who need the meaning, not the method.

Relevance

This artifact shows a different skill from my first two. Artifact 1 placed AI developments in context, and Artifact 2 applied technical understanding to a project decision. This one is about communication: explaining a technical idea so a beginner can follow it, which the course treats as a core part of leading change around AI. It supports my personal value proposition of bringing AI into research in a way that is explained in plain language and honest about its limits.
