Title

Before the Model: A Data-Challenge Checklist for Insights Teams

Introduction

Research teams now feed review text, customer records and survey data into machine learning models, and most model failures start with the data rather than the algorithm. This artifact comes from my Workshop 4 assignment, an interactive exercise with an AI coach that set three realistic data problems: cleaning messy customer reviews, scaling a résumé-screening model to a new country, and two hospitals building a model together without sharing raw records. I turned the decisions I made in that exercise into a short checklist an insights team can run before it trains, scales or shares a model.

Description

The page has four parts: a diagram showing where each data challenge appears in a model’s life; the three scenarios with the decisions I made; the checklist itself, with a question for each part that maps it to everyday research work; and a table showing where my first instinct was wrong and how I changed it. The coach’s own summary of my exercise is included at the end of the checklist, unedited, as evidence of the original work.

1 · Messy customer reviews

800,000 public reviews with duplicates, bot spam, emoji-only posts and labels taken from star ratings.

My decisions: spot bots with several signals (purchase badges, photos, repeated names, posting times); flag near-duplicates for review instead of deleting them; give every review an ID; lower the weight of unverified reviews with a “trust score” instead of removing them; check accuracy group by group.

2 · A model moving to a new market

A résumé screener trained on ten years of one company’s hiring decisions, now going to a new country at ten times the volume.

My decisions: expect local résumé styles to break the model; send uncertain cases to a human; run a pilot first as a “litmus test”; count the cost of errors to applicants and to the company.

3 · Sharing sensitive data

Two hospitals want one diagnostic model, but neither wants to hand over raw patient records, and they code data differently.

My decisions: mask and generalize personal details (age ranges, county instead of ZIP code); train without moving raw data; keep a shared code-translation “logbook”; treat hacking as a remaining risk; don’t let accuracy alone decide.

The checklist

Three short lists, one for each stage where things go wrong. Each ends with a question that maps it to everyday research work.

Clean without erasing real customers

  • Keep an untouched copy of the raw data and give every record an ID, so every change can be traced.
  • Flag near-duplicates and likely bots for review; don’t delete them on one rule.
  • Down-weight doubtful records instead of dropping them.
  • Keep emoji-only and other-language responses and label them by meaning; unusual is not the same as invalid.
  • Group duplicates before splitting training and test data. Copies on both sides inflate the test score.
  • Check results for each customer group, not only overall. A group’s share can look stable while its results get worse.

In research terms: before coding open-ended answers or social-listening data with AI, ask what our cleaning rules are quietly removing.

Scale only with local evidence

  • Treat past decisions used as labels (who was hired, who was targeted) as possibly biased, not as neutral truth.
  • Run a small pilot in the new market and check results group by group before full launch.
  • Send low-confidence cases to a person instead of forcing an automatic answer.
  • Write down who is hurt by each type of error, and how much.

In research terms: before taking a segmentation or targeting model built in one country into another, ask what evidence shows it still fits.

Share without exposing people

  • Share only what the model needs; mask names and generalize details such as age and location.
  • Prefer setups where the model travels and the raw data stays put.
  • Agree on one mapping table when partners code the same thing differently.
  • Agree in writing what the shared data and the finished model may be used for, and that neither partner can sell or reuse it alone.
  • Name the risks that remain (for example, hacking) and test the model at each partner separately.

In research terms: before combining client data with panel or agency data, ask who may use the combined result, and for what.

Objective

The Workshop 4 assignment asked me to work through three data-challenge scenarios with an AI coach, giving a decision and my reasoning each time, and to reflect on what I learned. Its course objectives were to discuss approaches for data processing and cleaning, and to explain the challenges of data sharing, privacy and security in machine learning. For the portfolio, my objective was to turn those decisions into a checklist that a research team can use before it relies on a model.

Process

  1. Ran the exercise: pasted the course’s “Data Challenge Scenarios Coach” prompt into Claude and worked through the three scenarios in order, answering each question and follow-up myself.
  2. Revised when challenged: changed my position on emoji-only reviews and on duplicate leakage after the coach’s follow-up questions.
  3. Kept the evidence: copied the coach’s concluding summary without changes and wrote a short reflection for the assignment.
  4. Grouped my decisions by the stage of a model’s life where each problem appears: cleaning, scaling and sharing.
  5. Rewrote them as checklist items a non-specialist can act on, and added a research-work question to each group.
  6. Revisited my weakest area: the coach flagged governance as unresolved, so the sharing list now includes agreeing how a jointly built model may be used.
  7. Built this page and checked it against the portfolio artifact template.

Value Proposition

This checklist gives insights teams a short, practical set of questions to ask before they train, scale or share a model, so data problems get caught at the start instead of after a client has seen the results.

Unique Value

Most data-quality checklists are written for engineers. This one is written for research teams and keeps real customers in view: it treats unusual responses as signal rather than noise, asks for results by group, and adds the governance question that research partnerships often skip. It also shows its own learning, including where my first instinct was wrong, which reflects how I want to bring AI into research: tested, explained and honest about limits.

Relevance

This artifact adds a skill my first three do not show. Artifact 1 placed AI developments in context, Artifact 2 applied technical understanding to a project decision, and Artifact 3 explained a technical idea to beginners. This one is about judging data before trusting a model: cleaning, bias, privacy and governance, which are the Workshop 4 outcomes. It connects directly to research work, where survey, review and customer data are the raw material of every insight.
