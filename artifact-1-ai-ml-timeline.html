Title

AI & ML Milestones for Insights Teams: A 1950–2024 Timeline

Introduction

Research and brand teams now hear “AI-powered” attached to almost everything: automatic coding of open-ended answers, predictive driver models, chatbot interviews and synthetic respondents. Some of these ideas are new. Many are decades old with a new interface. This timeline traces twelve milestones from 1950 to 2024 so that an insights team can see where each tool came from and ask better questions before trusting it.

Description

The artifact is an illustrated timeline of twelve milestones grouped into four eras: foundations, learning from data, deep learning, and generative AI with regulation. Every entry has three parts: what happened, a plain-English explanation, and a short note on why it matters for research work. Each milestone is tied to its original paper or official source, listed under References. A one-page version is available as a PDF for use in team meetings or onboarding.

The timeline

Foundations · 1950–1966
1950

Turing asks whether machines can think

Alan Turing proposed the “imitation game”: if a person cannot tell a machine’s written answers from a human’s, we have reason to call the machine intelligent.

Why it matters for research: We still judge AI output by whether it sounds human, for example AI-written open-end summaries or synthetic respondents. Sounding human is not the same as being accurate, so the output still has to be checked against real data.

Source: Turing (1950)
1955–56

The Dartmouth proposal names the field

McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester and Shannon wrote the 1955 proposal for a 1956 summer workshop and used the term “artificial intelligence.” They expected major progress in a single summer.

Why it matters for research: That early optimism still shows up in vendor pitches. Knowing how long real progress took helps a research team ask what a tool can actually do today.

Source: McCarthy et al. (1955/2006)
1958

Rosenblatt’s perceptron learns from examples

The perceptron adjusted its own weights after seeing labeled examples, instead of following hand-written rules.

Why it matters for research: This is the basic idea behind automated coding of survey answers: show the model coded examples and let it learn the pattern.

Source: Rosenblatt (1958)
1959

Samuel’s checkers program improves with practice

Arthur Samuel built a checkers program that got better by playing games, and his paper helped popularize the phrase “machine learning.”

Why it matters for research: It is an early case of a computer learning from experience, the same logic as a model that improves as more survey waves or transactions come in.

Source: Samuel (1959)
1966

ELIZA, the first well-known chatbot

Joseph Weizenbaum’s ELIZA matched keywords and reflected users’ words back to them. Many users still felt it understood them.

Why it matters for research: A direct warning for chatbot-led interviews and AI moderators: a fluent reply does not mean the system understood the respondent.

Source: Weizenbaum (1966)
Learning from data · 1986–2001
1986

Backpropagation makes deep networks trainable

Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams showed how errors can be passed backward through a network to train layers of hidden units.

Why it matters for research: Almost every neural model used in text and image analytics today is trained this way.

Source: Rumelhart et al. (1986)
2001

Random forests

Leo Breiman combined many decision trees, each built on a random sample of the data, and let them vote. The method also reports which variables matter most.

Why it matters for research: For driver-style questions (what is linked to satisfaction, switching or purchase intent), this gives analysts a model that handles messy survey data and still shows which variables carry weight.

Source: Breiman (2001)
Deep learning · 2012–2017
2012

Deep learning wins ImageNet

Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton trained a deep convolutional network on GPUs and beat other image-recognition entries by a wide margin.

Why it matters for research: Image recognition is now used to read shelf photos, packaging and ad creative, which used to need manual review.

Source: Krizhevsky et al. (2012)
2013

Word2vec turns words into numbers

Mikolov and colleagues showed how to represent words as vectors so that words used in similar ways sit close together.

Why it matters for research: This made it practical to group open-ended answers by meaning, not just exact keywords, for example “too pricey” and “expensive.”

Source: Mikolov et al. (2013)
2017

The transformer architecture

Vaswani and colleagues introduced a model built on “attention,” which looks at how every word in a passage relates to every other word.

Why it matters for research: Transformers are the foundation of today’s large language models; the “T” in GPT stands for transformer.

Source: Vaswani et al. (2017)
Generative AI and regulation · 2022–2024
2022

ChatGPT is released to the public

OpenAI released ChatGPT as a free research preview on November 30, 2022.

Why it matters for research: Large language models moved from labs to everyone’s desk: analysts, clients and respondents. Expectations for how fast insights can be produced changed almost overnight.

Source: OpenAI (2022)
2024

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act

The European Union adopted Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, a risk-based law for AI systems. It was published on July 12, 2024 and entered into force on August 1, 2024.

Why it matters for research: Teams using AI with EU customers or data need to know which risk category their use falls into and what transparency it requires.

Source: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (2024)

Objective

Workshop 1 asked us to build a timeline of how AI and machine learning developed. I had two goals. The first was my own: I wanted a solid picture of the field’s history before going deeper into the program. The second was for my audience. Insights managers, brand managers and research agency teams are being asked to adopt AI tools quickly, often without time to look into where those tools came from. I wanted to give them a short reference they could actually use when a new tool lands on their desk.

Process

  1. Started from my Workshop 1 work. I used the AI and ML timeline I prepared for the 1.3 discussion as the starting point.
  2. Picked an audience and a question. I chose research and brand teams and asked one question of every milestone: does it help explain a tool these teams use today? Milestones that did not pass were cut.
  3. Grouped the milestones into four eras so a reader can see the field move from rules, to learning from data, to deep learning, to generative AI.
  4. Checked every date and claim against the original source. Where dates differ, I show both. For example, the Dartmouth proposal was written in 1955 for a workshop held in 1956.
  5. Wrote a “why it matters for research” note for each entry, drawing on my own work in brand tracking, survey design and retail measurement.
  6. Built the web version and a one-page PDF, then reviewed both against the course artifact template and the rubric.

Value Proposition

This timeline gives insights and brand teams a five-minute way to place any “AI-powered” research tool in context, so they can ask vendors sharper questions and explain AI choices to stakeholders without overstating what the technology does.

Unique Value

Most AI timelines are written from a computer science point of view. This one is written from inside market research. Each milestone is paired with a practical consequence for research, such as what ELIZA suggests about chatbot interviews or what word embeddings changed for coding open-ended answers. That pairing comes from more than twelve years of running brand health, retail and survey studies, and it is the part a general timeline does not offer.

Relevance

The artifact supports my personal value proposition: bringing AI into research carefully, with each claim checked and explained in plain language. It is relevant now because research teams are adopting generative AI faster than they are learning how it works, and the EU AI Act has made it a compliance question as well as a quality question. For readers, the timeline is a starting point for judging new tools. For me, it is the base for the rest of this program, where I expect to work hands-on with several of the methods listed here.
